## What they say
- Pirates! (Firaxis): "is it any fun?" and "does it fit in our heroic world?" were the two filters. They chose a romanticised cinematic tone: "In our game, caution doesn't pay; the big rewards fall to the pirate with the most guts." They cut housekeeping ("What fun is it to have to scrape barnacles off of the bottom of your ship?") and plague. A fat merchant is the tempting target, a frigate is danger.
- Black Flag: broadsides, chain shot, fire barrels, mortars have distinct jobs by direction; you must position for a broadside. Wind was cut back deliberately: Ismail: "In earlier prototypes we had wind playing a big factor... it was way too complicated to be thinking about that as well as shooting. It just wasn't fun." Instead waves and storms are cover, and weather is a tactical tool. Boarding: you disable a ship, grapple, and fight on its deck; players love the loot and spectacle but say boarding gets "tedious" because only "3 pre determined layouts" existed, and that sinking was faster. The ship upgrade loop drove playtime (Ismail).
- FTL: the fantasy was "what would it feel like to be the captain of a starship"; crew, damage control, fires and pausing are the fun, and a random event at each stop.
- Cobalt Core: positioning as a shared axis; enemies are questions; crew-specific cards give personality; characters talk.
- Sea of Thieves (per summaries): playful tone, roles on the ship, shanties, grog.
- Dredge: cargo space as a spatial risk (summary); Darkest Dungeon: stress as a visible resource with afflictions or virtues.
- Pirates Outlaws: ship upgrades chosen per run, with reputation for slow meta grind (summaries).

## Takeaways for Free Nassau (the pirate fantasy has to live in the turn)
1. Wind as one simple lever, not a simulation: show a wind compass; each turn the wind favours Gunnery (Windward: broadside +2), Sail (Leeward: +Block cards), or neither. Cards and Captain orders can "Haul the wind" to flip it for a cost. This mirrors Black Flag's lesson.
2. Position: a "Range" track: Long, Close, Alongside. Ship cards have range requirements; Close enables boarding; Alongside lets boarding happen. Sail cards move range. This is Cobalt Core's single-axis tactics, in pirate clothes, and is the "positioning as a real lever" ask.
3. Captain Orders, one per turn, free, with a cooldown: "Fire at will" (all gun cards +2 this turn), "Hard to port" (dodge: next broadside -50%), "Run out the guns" (draw 2, powder -1), "Prepare to board" (set range Close), "Parley" (merchant surrenders for plunder; limited). That is the missing command verb from R2-8.
4. Enemies as questions plus a visible tell: Merchant (tempting, runs), Privateer, Hunter, Fireship, Galleon, Sloop-of-war, Guarda Capitana, Navy Warship, Pirate Hunter Squadron, Fort.
5. Boarding variety: three boarding "decks" (Open Deck: rush; Gun Deck: powder risk, grenades; Captain's Cabin: duel the captain) chosen by the enemy ship type, so boarding doesn't repeat Black Flag's three layouts problem. Crew shown as a visible little crowd of figures that thins out.
6. Cinematic tone: Errol Flynn heroism over grim realism, in line with the Pirates! designers. Officers speak barks. Navy is the villain.
7. Plunder-or-glory: merchants give the most plunder but little glory; navy ships give Renown. "Caution doesn't pay": extraction should be a strategic gamble, not a free exit.
